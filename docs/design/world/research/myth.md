# Research: myth and craft for the behemoth world (2026-11-23)

Caveat from the researcher: three searches, snippets only; "secondary" items come from snippets, "unconfirmed" from background knowledge.

## Motifs

- The island that is a beast (secondary): sailors land on a plant-covered island, light a fire, the creature dives. Aspidochelone; Jasconius (St Brendan); Fastitocalon (Old English); Lyngbakr (sagas); Imap Umassoursa (Greenland Inuit). Useful structure: a person misreads a living thing as land, and harm follows. Sources: Wikipedia "Aspidochelone"; bestiary.ca beast282.
- Sleeping heroes under hills (secondary): Thompson motif A571, "Kyffhäuser type": Arthur, Barbarossa, Thomas the Rhymer; "great king" and "last king" types; wakes when a horn or a need calls. Maps onto behemoths-as-hills: sleep, then a promised return. Sources: Wikipedia "King asleep in mountain"; Pitt "Sleeping Hero Legends"; Medievalists.net 2022.
- Giants who become land (unconfirmed): Ymir, Pangu, Irish/British landscape legends (Finn McCool), Japanese Daidarabotchi footprints making lakes.
- World-bearing animals (unconfirmed): Akupara, Genbu, Turtle Island; encircling beasts (Bakunawa, Jörmungandr, Leviathan). Use only the idea that the world rests on a living thing.
- Themes: sleep; burden (the creature carries a world); debt (the carried owe the carrier); return (home, or waking); the land remembering (the shape of the ground is a body or a grave).

## Craft

- Le Guin, "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (secondary): style is the whole effect of fantasy; avoid ordinary modern speech dressed up as Elfland (and its reverse, faux-archaic thee/thou).
- Earthsea: true names, plain sentences, balance. Dunsany: invented names, no explanation. Borges' Book of Imaginary Beings: dry scholarly entries quoting doubtful authorities. Calvino's Invisible Cities: each place one idea and one image. One concrete detail beats a paragraph of lore (unconfirmed).
- Miyazaki: nature spirits neither good nor evil; people and the living world both partly right; the world has its own scale; quiet moments carry feeling (unconfirmed).
- Unreliable legends: let legends disagree and never confirm which is true.

## Folk calendar and ritual (unsearched; analogues)

Wayside shrines (jizō, roadside crosses); pilgrim routes with waystations; transhumance festivals marking herds' departure and return; beating the bounds; hill figures re-chalked by the community (Uffington White Horse scouring); place- and season-bound taboos.

## Ten ideas

1. A behemoth at home does not die; it "settles", and its garden becomes a hill-meadow whose flowers still follow the old walking seasons.
2. Saying: "Never light a fire on a still hill" (from the island-beast tale): don't build where you don't know the ground.
3. Settling Day: each household climbs the nearest hill and leaves seeds from its own garden; the hill is owed its garden back.
4. Collar-makers' ruins hold iron rings too wide for any smith's tongs; legends disagree whether the ancients were jailers, shepherds or lovers.
5. The mill city grinds behemoth bone and horn into "hill-flour" and keeps a rival myth that hills are only hills.
6. Wayside cairns mark where a behemoth rested in earlier years; travellers add a stone for each safe night.
7. Each region names the same behemoth differently and tells a different story of why it walks.
8. A "listening" hill is a sleeper that stirs when its name is spoken nearby, so locals avoid saying it.
9. The creature's habits set the calendar: first-bloom, low-moult, the quiet week.
10. A village keeps a horn said to wake a hill; nobody knows whether it works.

## Cautions

- Indigenous material (songlines, the Rainbow Serpent, Turtle Island stories, Inuit stories) is living and sacred: themes only, never names, plots or sacred geography; invent our own pilgrim-route terms.
- Clichés: "noble primitive" ancients; a simply evil industrial city (give the mill city motives players can respect); faux-archaic speech; lore dumps.
- Real faith: Behemoth and Leviathan are biblical; avoid the bestiary Satan allegory; original names give distance.
- Hunting: keep the mill city's hunting tone considered (real feelings about whaling and animal welfare).
