# Research: folk calendars, wayside ritual, landscape memory, weather lore (2026-11-23)

All sources Wikipedia (secondary), opened in full. Not covered: bird-migration lore, the Sámi eight-season calendar, "the land remembers" as a motif.

## Folk calendars and festivals

Sources: Almabtrieb, Transhumance, Sámi people, First Fruits (Wikipedia).

- Almabtrieb: annual Alpine cattle drive in late September/early October; cattle decorated for the descent (decoration linked to a summer without accidents, by inference).
- Polish redyk: "the greatest village festival"; fire-passing, herb fumigation, sheep counting.
- Italian tratturi (drove roads) up to 100 m wide and 100 km long, public property by law.
- Alm/Alp words often became place names. First-fruits offerings as a fixed fraction of the harvest.
  Ideas: the road as a legal commons villages must keep open; garlands on the creature at a settlement showing a stretch without losses (a bare creature is a bad sign); first fruits or a counted tithe offered to the passing creature (counting rite, smoke passage: visual, no text); calendar days for departure, midpoint, homecoming; migration as sacred and fragile (Sámi themes only).
  Sensitivity: the Sámi are a living people under real pressure; themes only, no terms, dress or rituals, no villain or "vanishing" tropes. Alpine and Polish rituals are living festivals; abstract them.

## Wayside and route ritual

Sources: Wayside shrine, Cairn, Kumano Kodō, Beating the bounds, Uffington White Horse.

- Wayside shrines often honour an accident's victim; they also mark pilgrim routes and boundaries.
- Cairns mark paths across barren ground; people add a stone ("I'll put a stone on your cairn").
- Kumano's oji: a chain of subsidiary shrines along the route; one marks the formal entrance; purification at springs and in salt water.
- Beating the bounds: walking the parish boundary, a feast after; sometimes every seven years.
- Uffington horse: scoured about every seven years at a fair; it fades without upkeep.
  Ideas: each party adds a stone to a milestone cairn, which grows across seasons; a numbered chain of waystations to a threshold gate with a purification step; walking the bounds to remember the map; a figure that fades if nobody tends it (care and neglect without text); memorial shrines where earlier escorts fell.
  Sensitivity: Kumano, ovoo and ahu are living sacred sites; copy no names or forms. Leave out the boy-beating of the bounds.

## Landscape memory

Sources: Daidarabotchi, Giant's Causeway, Ymir, Pangu, Toponymy.

- Daidarabotchi: footprints made lakes and ponds; heaped shells into a hill; a benevolent giant; sometimes poses as a sleeping mountain range.
- Giant's Causeway: Fionn's causeway legend vs. cooled basalt (Fionn is a hero, not a giant, in Irish myth).
- Ymir and Pangu: a body becomes the world (bones mountains, blood rivers, breath wind).
- Folk etymology arises when a name's origin is forgotten.
  Ideas: the behemoths' ancestors shaped the land (lakes are footprints, a ridge a sleeping relative); rival origin stories for the same feature; place names carrying a half-forgotten event with a wrong folk explanation; the dead creature becoming the map as faint foreshadowing.
  Sensitivity: name no real gods or heroes.

## Weather and omen lore

Source: Weather lore.

- Red sky at night probably means dry weather (direction matters); cows lying down before rain is not reliable; seagulls huddled may mean bad weather is already here; many predictions persist by selective memory.
  Ideas: the behemoth itself as the weather tell (lies down, turns its back, goes still); village omens partly true, so reading them is a skill; omen cards tied to roles (Scout reads sky, Keeper reads animals), icons not prose; each settlement words the same sign differently.
